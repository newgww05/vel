Why Sacrococcygeal Redness
Isn't Always a Pressure Injury

From differentiating IAD and pressure injury to caring for patients when both occur together — when you see skin that's "red, wet, and broken," how do you work out what you're actually dealing with?

📅 Published August 17, 2026 · ⏱ 10 min read · 📊 1 table, bedside checklist
"

When you see skin that's "red, wet, and broken," the real question isn't "what does it look like" — it's "how did it happen."

— Core thesis of this article · IAD × Pressure Injury Differentiation
Key Takeaways

Five Key Points, at a Glance

  1. Sacrococcygeal redness ≠ automatically a pressure injury. The sacrococcygeal area is both a classic pressure-bearing zone and a moisture-exposed zone — a "crossroads" of two problems.
  2. IAD comes from moisture, pressure injury comes from pressure. IAD stems from the skin barrier breaking down after repeated exposure to urine/stool; pressure injury stems from sustained pressure/shear force combined with insufficient tissue tolerance.
  3. Both can occur at once. In a patient who is both incontinent and on prolonged bed rest, the sacrococcygeal area may carry both injury mechanisms at the same time — you don't have to pick just one.
  4. Differentiate by asking "why," not by memorizing staging charts. Three questions: Is there incontinence/moisture? Is there sustained pressure? Which mechanism does the injury pattern better fit?
  5. Care follows a "parallel decision-making" path. Address incontinence and skin barrier protection on one side, pressure redistribution and wound assessment on the other — both run in parallel, neither replaces the other.
Chapter 01 · Clinical Scenario

Don't Jumpto Conclusions

The most common bedside scene might go something like this:

A patient on prolonged bed rest has a patch of redness on the sacrococcygeal area. The skin is somewhat moist, and part of it has already broken down.

Someone glances at it and says: "Pressure injury."

That's not an unreasonable first read. The sacrococcygeal area is a classic pressure-bearing zone.

But what if this patient also has urinary or fecal incontinence?

That's when things get complicated.

That patch of red, eroded skin could be related to Incontinence-Associated Dermatitis (IAD); at the same time, prolonged bed rest and local pressure make pressure injury a real possibility too.

Here's the tricky part: these two things don't require you to choose one. They can happen at the same time, in the same anatomical area.

So what this article really wants to discuss isn't "how to memorize the differences between IAD and pressure injury," but a more practical question:

When you see skin that's "red, wet, and broken," how do you work out what you're actually dealing with?

Chapter 02 · Pathological Mechanism

Why Does One Patch of Red Skin
Point in Two Directions?

Because what our eyes see is the result, not the cause.

Redness, erosion, epidermal stripping, pain — these are all signs of damage that has already occurred. But the causes behind these signs can be completely different.

IAD is more like the skin barrier gradually breaking down after repeated exposure to urine, stool, and moisture.

Pressure injury, on the other hand, is more closely tied to sustained or repetitive pressure, shear force, and the tissue's own tolerance.

Both can ultimately make the skin "look bad," but they arrive there by different routes.

So the real question in differential diagnosis isn't "what does it look like" — it's "how did it happen."

Chapter 03 · Anatomical Characteristics

The Sacrococcygeal Area Is Especially
Easy to Misread

If a wound develops on the sacrococcygeal area, it's natural to think of pressure first. That's not wrong.

The problem is that the sacrococcygeal area is also the region most exposed to urine, stool, and moisture in incontinent patients.

In other words, this is precisely the "crossroads" of two problems.

So "sacrococcygeal location" can only tell us where the risk sits — it can't tell us directly what the injury actually is.

That's also why the statement "the sacrococcygeal area is red, so it's a pressure injury" can be too simplistic in clinical practice.

Chapter 04 · Bedside Thinking

Ask Three Questions First — More Useful
Than Reaching for a Staging Chart

Question 1: Is there incontinence and moisture here?

Is there urinary incontinence? Fecal incontinence? Is the skin frequently moist? Is cleansing frequent? Is wiping forceful?

These details may seem trivial, but they can actually change the judgment.

Question 2: Is there sustained pressure or shear force here?

Is the patient on prolonged bed rest? Can they reposition themselves? Is sitting time long? Is there noticeable shear force during moving or transferring?

If these factors are clearly present, the skin problem can't simply be understood as superficial irritation.

Question 3: Could both problems be true?

This is the question most worth remembering.

If the patient is both incontinent and on prolonged bed rest, the answer could well be "yes."

Chapter 05 · Differentiation Tool

Don't Turn Differential Diagnosis Into
"Guess the Answer by Color"

Both IAD and pressure injury can present with redness, pain, and skin breakdown. Judging by color alone can easily lead you into a dead end.

But the table below isn't an "automatic diagnostic tool." Clinical judgment isn't finished once you've ticked all five boxes. Its real purpose is to remind us not to overlook the mechanism behind what we're seeing.

What to Observe Points More Toward IAD Points More Toward Pressure Injury
Exposure history Repeated exposure to urine, stool, or moisture Prolonged pressure, limited mobility, or shear force
Distribution Often more diffuse, irregular borders, commonly involves buttocks/perineum Often linked to pressure-bearing areas and bony prominences
Skin presentation Moisture, maceration, erosion, and epidermal stripping are prominent May show redness and breakdown, possibly with deeper tissue damage
Care clues Frequent cleansing, difficulty managing incontinence Reduced ability to reposition, prolonged sitting/lying, insufficient offloading
The most critical question Has the irritant source been controlled? Has pressure actually been relieved or redistributed?
Chapter 06 · Common Pitfalls

The Two Most Common
Mistakes

Common Mistake 01

Seeing the sacrococcygeal area and assuming pressure injury by default

This is the most intuitive judgment, which is exactly why it happens so easily. But if a large part of the real problem stems from incontinence and moisture, offloading pressure alone won't solve the skin barrier problem.

Common Mistake 02

Seeing moisture and erosion and attributing it all to IAD

This is another form of "surface-only" judgment. If the patient is also under prolonged pressure, epidermal erosion doesn't mean deeper tissue is free of pressure-related damage.

The two mistakes look like opposites, but they actually share one thing in common: both compress a complex clinical problem into a single label.

Chapter 07 · Complex Scenario

The Truly Tricky Situation:
IAD and Pressure Injury Together

Suppose a patient is on prolonged bed rest and also has urinary incontinence. The sacrococcygeal area is under sustained pressure, and the skin is repeatedly exposed to moisture.

In this case, the skin barrier may already be compromised by IAD, while sustained pressure creates a second, separate injury pathway.

If we insist on asking "is this IAD or pressure injury," the question itself may be the wrong one to ask.

A better question is: "how many injury mechanisms are happening here at the same time?"

Once you think this way, the care that follows actually becomes much clearer.

Chapter 08 · Care Strategy

Two Care Pathways, Running in Parallel

Route A

If it's mainly IAD

Reduce the skin's contact time with urine, stool, and moisture. Cleanse gently, minimizing repeated friction. Apply proper skin barrier protection. Address the source of incontinence and contamination at the same time.

Core: control the irritant + protect the barrier
Route B

If it's mainly pressure injury

Relieve pressure and redistribute it appropriately. Assess the extent and depth of the injury. Provide local wound care matched to the wound's condition. Keep observing for change rather than relying on a single look.

Core: offload pressure + assess tissue + monitor over time
Route A + B

If both are present

Don't choose one approach over the other. Address incontinence, moisture, and skin barrier protection on one track, while managing pressure redistribution, tissue assessment, and wound care on the other.

Core: parallel decision-making, parallel intervention

This is what's known as "parallel decision-making": it's not about one diagnosis winning out over the other, but about letting each intervention address its own corresponding cause.

Chapter 09 · Practical Tool

A Simple
Bedside Thinking Sequence

  1. Is there urine, stool, or persistent moisture here?
  2. Is there sustained pressure or shear force here?
  3. Does the distribution of the injury better fit one mechanism over the other?
  4. Is there evidence suggesting both mechanisms are present?
  5. Is the care I'm giving right now actually addressing the cause, or just treating the surface?
Chapter 10 · Ongoing Observation

One Last Question That's Easy to Overlook:
Change Over Time

A single observation is just a snapshot in time.

Judgments of real value usually come from continuous observation: Has the affected area grown larger? Has the redness changed? Has moisture decreased? Has the condition improved after offloading pressure? Has the patient's pain or discomfort changed?

If the injury keeps expanding, pain increases significantly, blistering, necrosis, or abnormal exudate appears, or deeper tissue involvement is suspected, it should no longer simply be managed as IAD — a timely professional assessment is needed.

Don't Rush to Label the Skin

IAD and pressure injury are easy to confuse not because there's no difference between them, but because the problems presented by real patients are rarely that "clean-cut."

The same patient can be incontinent, bed-bound, and have limited mobility all at once; the same sacrococcygeal area can bear both moisture and pressure at the same time.

So truly mature clinical judgment isn't slapping a label on redness the moment you see it — it's pausing to ask:

"Why did this skin end up looking the way it does now?"

Once we start from the cause, differentiating IAD from pressure injury stops being a matter of memorizing a few distinctions and becomes a genuinely useful clinical reasoning process.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Is sacrococcygeal redness always a pressure injury?
Not necessarily. The sacrococcygeal area is a classic pressure-bearing zone, but it's also the area most exposed to urine, stool, and moisture in incontinent patients. Redness can come from IAD, pressure injury, or both at once. The key is to ask about the cause rather than judging by appearance alone.
What's the difference between IAD and pressure injury?
IAD results from the skin barrier gradually breaking down after repeated exposure to urine, stool, and moisture; pressure injury is more closely tied to sustained or repetitive pressure, shear force, and the tissue's own tolerance. Both can cause redness, erosion, and pain, but the underlying causes differ. IAD tends to be more diffuse with irregular borders; pressure injury is more often linked to bony prominences under pressure.
Can IAD and pressure injury occur at the same time?
Yes, and often in the same area. A patient who is both incontinent and on prolonged bed rest may have sacrococcygeal skin subjected to moisture irritation and sustained pressure simultaneously. Rather than getting stuck on "which one is it," clinicians should identify every injury mechanism at play and apply matching interventions in parallel.
How do you tell IAD and pressure injury apart?
Differential diagnosis isn't about color — it's about asking why: ① Is there incontinence or moisture? ② Is there sustained pressure or shear force? ③ Does the distribution pattern fit one mechanism better than another? ④ Is there evidence both mechanisms are present? For reference: IAD often involves the buttocks/perineum, has irregular borders, and shows prominent moisture-associated maceration; pressure injury is often linked to bony prominences and may involve deeper tissue damage.
How should you care for a patient when IAD and pressure injury occur together?
Use a "parallel decision-making" approach: address incontinence, moisture, and skin barrier protection on one track, while simultaneously managing pressure redistribution, tissue assessment, and wound care on the other. Advance both sets of measures together rather than choosing one over the other. This includes reducing contact time with urine/stool, gentle cleansing, skin barrier protectant products, a pressure redistribution plan, regular repositioning, and ongoing monitoring for change.
