Key Takeaways

Five Checks, Before You Sign

  1. Verify documents, don't just collect them. ISO 13485, CE and FDA claims can all be checked against public databases — and the scope must cover the exact products you are buying.
  2. "FDA registered" is not "FDA cleared." Registration is a facility listing; 510(k) clearance is product-specific. Know which one your market and product actually need.
  3. Evaluate samples against a checklist, not a feeling. Absorbency, adhesion, removal, pouch seal and labelling — compared side by side with the product you sell today.
  4. Settle who the legal manufacturer is before designing artwork. Private label changes regulatory responsibility, especially under EU MDR.
  5. Plan the first order three months ahead. Production, packaging approval and sea freight add up — and a trial order is the cheapest audit you will ever run.
Chapter 01 · Start With Your Market

Define What You Need
Before You Search

Most sourcing problems start before the first supplier email. A buyer asks ten factories for "foam dressing prices," receives ten quotes for ten different products, and ends up comparing numbers that were never comparable.

Before you contact anyone, write down four things:

  • Destination market(s). EU, UK, US, GCC, Latin America and Southeast Asia each have different registration requirements. This decides which documents matter.
  • Product specification. Dressing type (silicone foam, hydrocolloid, alginate, silver, hydrogel…), sizes, bordered or non-bordered, sterile or non-sterile, units per box.
  • Business model. Buying the supplier's own brand, buying stock product under your brand (OEM), or co-developing something new (ODM).
  • Volume and timing. Expected annual quantity per SKU and the date you need stock on your shelf.

A one-page brief with these four points will get you faster, more accurate quotes — and immediately shows serious manufacturers that you are a serious buyer.

Chapter 02 · Regulatory Due Diligence

Certificates: What to Ask For
and How to Verify It

Wound dressings are medical devices almost everywhere. That means the supplier's paperwork is not a formality — it is what allows you to legally sell the product. Three documents come up in nearly every deal.

ISO 13485:2016 — the quality system

ISO 13485 certifies the manufacturer's quality management system for medical devices. Check three things on the certificate: the issuing body (it should be accredited — you can search many certificates on IAF CertSearch), the scope (does it mention the design and manufacture of wound dressings, not just "trading"?), and the expiry date.

CE marking under EU MDR 2017/745

Under the EU Medical Device Regulation, wound dressings are classified mainly by Rule 4. Simple absorbent or barrier dressings can be Class I (self-declared by the manufacturer); dressings intended to manage the wound micro-environment are typically Class IIa; dressings for wounds that breach the dermis and heal only by secondary intent are Class IIb. Dressings incorporating a medicinal substance can be classified as Class III.

For anything above Class I, ask for the Notified Body certificate. It carries a four-digit Notified Body number you can look up in the EU's NANDO database, and it lists the product groups it covers. Non-EU manufacturers must also appoint an EU Authorised Representative, whose name appears on the label.

FDA — registration, listing and 510(k)

For the United States, the manufacturer needs establishment registration and device listing, searchable in the FDA's public database, and a US Agent if based abroad. Registration alone is not an approval. Many basic dressings are Class I and exempt from premarket review, but dressings with added antimicrobial agents (such as silver), collagen dressings and several advanced products typically require 510(k) clearance, identified by a K-number in the FDA 510(k) database.

If you sell into the UK, also ask how the product is registered with the MHRA and who acts as the UK Responsible Person.

Chapter 03 · Sample Evaluation

Evaluate Samples
Like an Auditor

Samples are where most buyers either save or lose the deal. The mistake is opening the pouch, touching the dressing and deciding "it feels good." Instead, test it side by side with the product you sell today — or the market leader your customers compare you to.

  1. Absorbency and retention: add a measured amount of saline, wait, then apply light pressure. Does the fluid stay locked in, or does it squeeze back out?
  2. Adhesion and removal: wear a bordered dressing for a day. Does it stay put at the edges? Does it lift cleanly without stripping skin or leaving residue?
  3. Conformability: apply it to a heel, elbow or sacrum shape. Does it wrinkle and channel fluid, or follow the contour?
  4. Pouch and seal: is the seal continuous, the peel clean, the sterile barrier intact after shipping?
  5. Labelling: lot number, expiry date, sterilisation method, size, manufacturer and representative details, and the correct symbols — all present and legible?

Then ask the supplier for the test reports behind every claim on the data sheet. A good manufacturer will send them without hesitation; a hesitant one has told you something important.

Chapter 04 · Choosing a Model

Stock, OEM or ODM:
Three Ways to Work With a Factory

Route A · Stock

Buy the manufacturer's product as-is

Fastest and lowest MOQ. The manufacturer's registrations usually cover the product directly, so your regulatory workload is lightest. Best for testing a market or filling a range gap.

Core: speed + low risk
Route B · OEM / Private Label

Existing product, your brand

Proven specification with your packaging, artwork and language. Higher MOQ and extra time for artwork approval — and you must agree who is the legal manufacturer on the label.

Core: brand ownership + proven product
Route C · ODM

Co-develop a new product

New size, shape, formulation or configuration for your market. Longest timeline, because prototypes, testing and possibly new registrations are needed — but the result is hard for competitors to copy.

Core: differentiation + long-term value

The private-label question nobody asks early enough

When a dressing is sold under your brand, someone must be the legal manufacturer — the party responsible for technical documentation, vigilance and post-market surveillance. Under EU MDR Article 16, a company that places a device on the market under its own name can take on the manufacturer's obligations, unless the original manufacturer remains identified and responsible under an agreement.

Settle this in writing before you design the box. It determines what appears on the label, which registrations you need, and who handles a complaint. For each destination market, confirm the arrangement with a regulatory adviser.

Chapter 05 · Commercial Terms

MOQ, Lead Time and Logistics:
Plan the First Order Backwards

Three numbers decide whether your first order arrives on time: MOQ, production lead time and transit time. Ask for all three in writing, per SKU.

  • MOQ is usually set per SKU and rises with custom packaging. Ask whether a smaller trial order is possible for the first shipment.
  • Lead time normally starts from order confirmation and deposit — not from your first email. Custom artwork approval adds days; very large volumes add weeks.
  • Transit by sea commonly takes three to five weeks depending on the port, plus customs clearance. Express courier is for samples and emergencies.
  • Incoterms (EXW, FOB, CIF, DDP) decide who pays for and controls freight, insurance and import duties. Be explicit.
  • Shipping documents: commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin and the product certificates your customs authority requires.

Work backwards from the date you need stock. For a first OEM order, three months from final artwork to stock on your shelf is a realistic planning baseline.

Chapter 06 · Quality Control

Make the Tenth Shipment
Match the First Sample

A perfect sample proves the factory can make the product. Quality control proves it will, every time. Before scaling up, agree on:

  • A golden sample, signed off by both sides and kept as the reference for every future lot.
  • Inspection criteria: which defects are critical, major or minor, and the sampling plan — commonly AQL levels under ISO 2859-1.
  • Pre-shipment inspection: by your own staff or a third-party inspection company, before the balance payment.
  • Lot traceability: every box traceable to raw-material batches, the production date and the sterilisation load.
  • Complaint handling: how fast the manufacturer investigates, and whether it provides a written root-cause and corrective-action report.
Chapter 07 · Red Flags

Four Signs It's Time
to Walk Away

Red Flag 01

Certificates that can't be found — or don't name the product

A certificate with no traceable number, an expired date, or a scope that covers "trading" or an unrelated product. If you can't verify it yourself, treat it as missing.

Red Flag 02

"We can make anything" with no technical data

Every specification promised, but no data sheet, no test report, and no questions back about your market. Real manufacturers ask what you need before saying yes.

Red Flag 03

A price far below everyone else

In medical dressings, an unusually low price usually means thinner material, a different adhesive, skipped sterilisation validation — or a trading company re-selling from an unknown source.

Red Flag 04

Slow, vague answers before you have paid

Response speed and clarity before the order are the best they will ever be. If questions go unanswered now, complaints will go unanswered later.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What certifications should a wound dressing manufacturer have?
At minimum, an ISO 13485:2016 certificate from an accredited certification body. For the EU, the products need CE marking under EU MDR 2017/745, with a Notified Body certificate for Class IIa, IIb and III dressings. For the US, the manufacturer needs FDA establishment registration and device listing, and many advanced dressings also need 510(k) clearance. Always check that the certificate scope covers the exact products you are buying.
Is "FDA registered" the same as "FDA approved" or "FDA cleared"?
No. Registration means the facility is registered and its devices are listed; it is not an endorsement of the product. Many basic dressings are Class I and exempt from premarket review, while others need 510(k) clearance — a product-specific K-number you can search in the FDA 510(k) database.
What is a typical MOQ for OEM or private-label wound dressings?
It varies by product and packaging. At Velcura Medical, standard stock items typically start from 5,000 pieces per SKU, and OEM / private-label orders with custom packaging generally start from 10,000 pieces per SKU. Small trial orders can be discussed for first-time partners.
How long does it take to produce a wound dressing order in China?
Standard production commonly runs about 25–35 working days after order confirmation and deposit, with stock items dispatched faster. Custom packaging adds time for artwork approval, and sea freight typically adds 20–35 days. Plan a first order at least three months before you need stock.
Who is the legal manufacturer when I sell under my own brand?
It depends on the agreement and the market. Under EU MDR Article 16, a company placing a device on the market under its own name can take on the manufacturer's obligations unless the original manufacturer remains identified and responsible under an agreement. Settle this in writing before artwork is designed, and confirm it with a regulatory adviser.
How should I evaluate wound dressing samples?
Compare them side by side with the product you sell today: absorbency and retention, adhesion and atraumatic removal, conformability, pouch seal integrity and label content. Then ask for the test reports behind every data-sheet claim, ideally to EN 13726 for performance and ISO 10993 for biocompatibility.
