Hydrogel Dressings
The One That Works Backwards

Most advanced dressings exist to take moisture away from a wound — foam, alginate and super-absorbent fibres all compete to soak up as much exudate as possible. Hydrogel does the opposite. It is a soft, cross-linked network of polymer chains holding up to 90% water by weight, and its entire job is to give that water back to a wound that doesn't have enough of its own.

A dry, sloughy or necrotic wound can't heal by itself — the body's own enzymes need a moist environment to break down and clear dead tissue, a process called autolytic debridement. Hydrogel simply supplies the missing moisture and gets out of the way, letting the body do work it already knows how to do.

Three Formats, One Job

Same Science,
Three Different Shapes

Wounds aren't all flat, and they aren't all shallow. Velcura's hydrogel range covers the three physical situations a dry wound can present — a broad shallow surface, a general area needing gentle rehydration, and a deep cavity or tunnel that a sheet could never reach.

Sheet Format

Hydrogel Dressing

A pre-formed gel sheet that lays directly over the wound — self-retaining, non-adherent, and gentle enough to remove without pain even from fragile periwound skin.

Best for: flat or gently curved dry wounds with low exudate — pressure injuries, radiation skin reactions, minor burns and abrasions.
Amorphous Tube · General

Hydrogel

A flowable gel dispensed straight from a 30g tube — no applicator needed. Spreads easily over irregular surfaces for everyday cleansing and rehydration of dry, necrotic tissue.

Best for: general-purpose daily rehydration on dry or necrotic wounds, leg ulcers, and radiation skin reactions.
Amorphous Tube · Cavity

Hydrogel Dressing (Cavity)

The same amorphous gel logic, engineered with ≥80% water content specifically for precise placement deep into cavities, sinuses and tunnelled wound tracks that a sheet or a general-purpose tube can't reach.

Best for: deep cavity wounds, sinuses, tunnelled tracks — always paired with a secondary dressing.

Quick Decision Guide

Which One
Does This Wound Need?

Three questions, in order. The first one your wound answers "yes" to points to the right format.

Question 1

Is the wound flat and shallow?

A broad, gently curved surface with low exudate — no depth to fill, no cavity to pack.
→ Hydrogel Dressing (Sheet)
Question 2

Is it irregular but not deep?

Dry or necrotic tissue over an uneven surface that a flat sheet won't sit against evenly.
→ Hydrogel (General Tube)
Question 3

Does it have depth, a sinus or a tunnel?

Anywhere a fingertip could disappear into the wound — gel needs to be packed in, not laid on top.
→ Hydrogel Dressing (Cavity)

Frequently Asked Questions

Common Questions on
Hydrogel Dressings

What is hydrogel wound dressing used for?
Hydrogel dressings are used on dry, sloughy or necrotic wounds that need moisture added to the wound bed — the opposite job to absorbent dressings like foam or alginate. Common uses include pressure injuries with dry eschar, radiation skin reactions, minor burns, and any wound where autolytic debridement needs to be encouraged.
What is the difference between hydrogel sheet and amorphous hydrogel?
Sheet hydrogel is a pre-formed gel pad best suited to flat or gently curved wounds with low exudate — it can stay in place up to 3 days. Amorphous hydrogel is a flowable gel dispensed from a tube, allowing it to be packed into irregular, deep, cavity or tunnelled wounds where a sheet cannot conform. Amorphous formats always need a secondary dressing to hold them in place; sheet hydrogel does not.
Can hydrogel be used on infected wounds?
Plain hydrogel has no antimicrobial action of its own. It should not be relied on as the primary treatment for a clinically infected wound — a silver or antimicrobial dressing is usually indicated instead, sometimes in combination with debridement. Always assess infection status before selecting a dressing.
How often should a hydrogel dressing be changed?
Sheet hydrogel can typically remain in place for up to 3 days depending on wound condition. Amorphous tube hydrogel is generally reapplied daily to every 3 days, more frequently if the wound is producing more exudate than expected or if the secondary dressing needs changing. Always follow product labelling and the treating clinician's plan.
Does hydrogel dressing need a secondary dressing?
Amorphous (tube) hydrogel formats always require a secondary cover dressing — commonly foam, film or gauze — because the gel itself has no adhesive border and cannot stay in place alone. Sheet hydrogel is typically self-retaining for flat wound sites but may still be secured with a secondary dressing or tape depending on wound location.
