Pre-Surgical Skin Antisepsis
The Modern Protocol

Chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine? After two decades of trials, the more useful answer is: alcohol-based, applied properly and allowed to dry. Here is the protocol, the evidence behind it, and where iodine incise drapes honestly fit.

Key Takeaways

Five Things to Know in Two Minutes

  1. Alcohol-based preparations are the common thread of WHO and CDC skin-prep recommendations.
  2. CHG-alcohol outperformed aqueous povidone-iodine in a landmark trial (9.5% vs 16.1% SSI).
  3. Iodine-in-alcohol was non-inferior to CHG-alcohol in a large 2024 trial — the carrier matters most.
  4. Drying time is a safety step: alcohol is flammable until evaporated, and drapes do not stick to wet prep.
  5. Incise drapes help with field stability and fluid control; they are not, on their own, an SSI-reduction tool.
Chapter 01 · The Goal

What Skin Antisepsis
Is Really For

Most surgical site infections (SSI) start with the patient's own skin flora. Surgical skin preparation cannot sterilize skin — bacteria live in hair follicles and sebaceous glands beyond the reach of any solution — but it can drop the bacterial load sharply at the moment of incision and keep it low for the length of the operation.

That is why modern protocols judge an antiseptic on two things: how fast it kills (immediate effect) and how long it keeps working (persistent or residual effect). The best-performing preparations combine an alcohol for speed with a second agent for persistence.

The decisive word in that table is alcohol. The WHO global guidelines recommend alcohol-based antiseptic solutions based on CHG for surgical site preparation, and the US CDC guideline recommends an alcohol-based agent unless contraindicated. The landmark trial behind this shift (Darouiche, 2010) found SSI in 9.5% of patients prepped with CHG-alcohol versus 16.1% with aqueous povidone-iodine in clean-contaminated surgery.

Where does the evidence sit in 2026? The large Swiss randomized trial published in 2024 found iodine-in-alcohol non-inferior to CHG-alcohol. Read together with the earlier trials, the consensus is clear: the alcohol carrier matters more than the CHG-versus-iodine debate. Choose the agent by patient factors, surgical site and local protocol — but choose an alcohol-based one whenever it is safe.

Chapter 03 · Contact Time

Dry Is Part
of the Dose

Drying time is not a formality. Alcohol is flammable until it has evaporated, and pooled solution in skin folds, under the patient or soaked into linen is a known source of operating-room fires when electrosurgery is used. The product label gives the minimum dry time — typically a few minutes on hairless skin and longer on hairy areas — and it is a safety requirement, not a suggestion.

Dry time also matters for adhesion. Any drape, film dressing or securement device applied over wet antiseptic will lift at the edges.

Chapter 04 · Sequencing

Where Iodine Incise Drapes
Fit — Honestly

An incise drape is an adhesive film applied to prepped skin through which the surgeon cuts. An iodine incise drape adds an iodophor to the adhesive so antimicrobial activity continues at the skin surface during surgery.

It is worth being clear about the evidence. Systematic reviews have not shown that plastic adhesive drapes, with or without an antimicrobial, reduce SSI rates on their own, and the WHO guideline makes a conditional recommendation against using them for the purpose of SSI prevention. Surgeons still choose them for practical reasons:

  • A stable, sterile field that does not shift during long procedures.
  • Skin immobilization at the incision edge and fewer drape clips.
  • Fluid management — irrigation and blood run off instead of soaking linen.
  • Continuous antimicrobial contact at the skin surface where the drape is used.

If a drape is used, the sequence is simple: antiseptic → full drying time → drape applied smoothly without wrinkles or air pockets, edges well sealed. See the standard incise drape and the surgical wound care guide for the full perioperative range.

Error 01

Draping over wet prep

The drape lifts at the edges, the iodophor contact is lost and alcohol vapor is trapped under the film — an adhesion and a fire-safety problem at the same time.

Error 02

Shaving the site

Razors create micro-abrasions that bacteria colonize. If hair must be removed, use clippers, ideally just before surgery.

Error 03

Mixing agents on the same field

Layering CHG and iodine products without a protocol can reduce performance and complicates allergy tracing. Pick one system and follow its instructions.

Error 04

Rushing the scrub

Antiseptics need friction and coverage. A quick wipe over a large field leaves gaps exactly where the incision may be extended.

Did you know? Pre-operative bathing with plain or antimicrobial soap is recommended by WHO — the night-before shower is the first step of skin antisepsis, not a hotel courtesy.

A Protocol Is Only as Good as Its Slowest Step

The chemistry has largely been settled: alcohol-based antisepsis, applied with friction and coverage. What still varies from one operating room to the next is execution — clipping instead of shaving, giving the antiseptic its full drying time, and sealing drapes on dry skin.

Products support the protocol; they don't replace it. Choose drapes and dressings that perform well on correctly prepared skin.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Is chlorhexidine better than povidone-iodine for surgical skin prep?
Chlorhexidine-alcohol outperformed aqueous povidone-iodine in a landmark 2010 trial (SSI 9.5% vs 16.1%). However, a large 2024 trial found povidone-iodine in alcohol non-inferior to chlorhexidine-alcohol. The alcohol-based carrier is the most important factor.
How long should surgical skin prep dry before draping?
For the full minimum time stated on the product label — typically a few minutes on hairless skin and longer on hairy areas — and with no pooling. Alcohol is flammable until evaporated, and drapes adhere poorly to wet skin.
Do iodine incise drapes reduce surgical site infections?
Systematic reviews have not shown that adhesive incise drapes, with or without iodophor, reduce SSI on their own, and WHO conditionally recommends against using them for SSI prevention. They are used for field stability, skin immobilization and fluid management.
Should hair be shaved before surgery?
No. WHO and CDC advise against shaving; if hair must be removed, use clippers, ideally shortly before surgery, because razors cause micro-abrasions.
When is aqueous povidone-iodine still used?
On mucous membranes and in situations where alcohol is contraindicated, according to the product label and local protocol.
