Prophylactic Silicone Foam on the Sacrum
When to Apply

The 2026 International Guideline now names the dressing: multilayered soft silicone foam on the sacrum and heels for high-risk patients. Here is who qualifies, how the layers protect tissue, how long to wear it, and how to check the skin without breaking the seal.

Key Takeaways

Five Things to Know in Two Minutes

  1. The 2026 guideline names the product: multilayered soft silicone foam on sacrum and heels for high-risk individuals.
  2. It is a conditional recommendation with very low certainty — use it within a full prevention plan.
  3. No universal Braden cut-off: risk comes from screening, full assessment and clinical judgment.
  4. Inspect daily by lifting the silicone border; change when soiled, wet, wrinkled or displaced.
  5. Keep turning — typically every two to three hours, using 30-degree lateral positions.
Chapter 01 · What the Guideline Says

The 2026 Recommendation,
Word for Word

The 4th edition of the International Guideline (NPIAP / EPUAP / PPPIA, version September 2026) gives a specific answer to "which dressing, where, for whom":

Guideline SK2: "We suggest using a multilayered soft silicone foam dressing on sacrum and heels for individuals assessed as having a high risk of pressure injuries, where resources permit." — Conditional recommendation, very low certainty of evidence (SK2).

Three details matter. It names a specific construction — multilayered, soft silicone foam — rather than "a foam dressing". It targets high-risk individuals, not everyone. And it is conditional: most well-informed teams would use it, but the certainty of evidence is low, so local resources and judgment count. For heels, the guideline adds that a preventive dressing is an adjunct to heel elevation and repositioning — never a replacement.

The sacrum-shaped silicone border foam and the heel-shaped version are built to this multilayer design.

Chapter 03 · When to Apply

Which Braden Score?
The Honest Answer

There is no universal Braden cut-off in the guideline. The 4th edition asks for a risk screening at admission, a full risk assessment for anyone screened as likely at risk, and clinical judgment layered on top of any risk scale. Risk scales supplement assessment; they do not replace it.

In practice, many hospital protocols translate this into triggers like these — set locally, with your tissue-viability team:

Typical local triggerWhy it signals high risk
Admission to critical careImmobility, vasopressors, ventilation, poor perfusion — all named risk factors in the 2026 guideline
Braden score at or below the local threshold (often ≤ 12–16)Low mobility, moisture and friction sub-scores in particular
Long surgery or prolonged pre-operative immobilityThe guideline names surgery duration and time immobilized as OR risk factors
Existing pressure injury elsewhere, or non-blanching erythemaDemonstrated low tissue tolerance
Spinal cord injury, shock states, end-of-life careSpecial-population risk factors listed in the guideline
Chapter 04 · Daily Practice

Inspect Without
Breaking the Seal

  1. Apply on clean, completely dry skin, centered over the sacrum with the shaped lobes into the natal cleft — no stretching, no wrinkles.
  2. Inspect the skin at least daily and whenever risk changes: gently peel back the border, look and feel for redness, warmth, firmness or pain, then smooth it back down.
  3. Change it when soiled, wet underneath, wrinkled, displaced or lifting — and otherwise per the instructions for use and local policy.
  4. Keep repositioning. The guideline is explicit: no support surface or dressing replaces repositioning. Use an individualized schedule — typically every two to three hours on a pressure-redistributing mattress.
  5. "Switching sides" means 30-degree lateral turns, which offload both sacrum and trochanter. Larger patients may need about 40 degrees for real offloading.
  6. Document the dressing date, each skin check and the turning schedule.
Error 01

Using it instead of turning

A dressing reduces shear and friction; it does not remove pressure. The patient still needs repositioning and a suitable mattress.

Error 02

Leaving a soiled dressing in place

With incontinence, stool under the border traps irritants against skin. Cleanse, protect and replace.

Error 03

Never looking underneath

Soft silicone is designed to be lifted and re-applied. Skipping inspection hides the earliest — most reversible — damage.

Error 04

Any foam will do

The guideline names multilayered soft silicone foam. Single-layer foams and aggressive adhesives do not offer the same shear management or gentle removal.

A Small Dressing in a Bigger Plan

Prophylactic silicone foam is one of the few prevention tools with a named place in the 2026 guideline. Its value depends on the protocol around it: the right patients, daily inspection, timely changes and continued repositioning.

Get those right, and a few dollars of dressing protects against one of the most expensive complications in hospital care.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the 2026 guideline recommend prophylactic sacral dressings?
Yes. The 4th edition International Guideline suggests a multilayered soft silicone foam dressing on the sacrum and heels for individuals at high risk of pressure injuries, where resources permit (conditional recommendation, very low certainty of evidence).
At what Braden score should a prophylactic dressing be applied?
The guideline sets no universal Braden cut-off. Risk is determined by screening, a full risk assessment and clinical judgment. Hospitals commonly set local triggers such as ICU admission or a Braden score at or below a local threshold.
How long can a prophylactic sacral foam dressing stay on?
Follow the instructions for use and local policy. Change it whenever it is soiled, wet underneath, wrinkled, lifting or displaced, and inspect the skin underneath at least daily by gently lifting the silicone border.
Can a prophylactic dressing replace repositioning?
No. The guideline states that no support surface can replace repositioning, and dressings are adjuncts. Continue an individualized turning schedule, typically every two to three hours on a pressure-redistributing mattress.
Why silicone foam rather than hydrocolloid or film?
Multilayer silicone foam absorbs shear, spreads pressure, manages moisture and can be lifted for skin inspection without damaging skin — which is why the 2026 guideline names this construction.
