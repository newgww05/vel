NPIAP / EPUAP Guideline Update
What Changed

The 4th edition of the International Pressure Injury Guideline is now complete, with prevention and treatment in one Quick Reference Guide (version September 2026). Here is what changed — a new grading system, named prophylactic dressings, updated repositioning and support-surface advice — what stayed the same, and what it means for daily dressing decisions.

📅 Published November 21, 2026 · ⏱ 7 min read · 📊 1 chart, 2 summary tables
"

The 4th edition doesn't change what a pressure injury is. It changes how confident we are allowed to be about each thing we do to prevent one.

Key Takeaways

Five Things to Know in Two Minutes

  1. GRADE replaces A/B1/B2/C: read "recommend", "suggest", "no recommendation" and "good practice" literally.
  2. Prophylactic dressings are named: multilayer soft silicone foam on sacrum and heels for high-risk patients; strong advice for dressings under medical devices.
  3. Repositioning: 2- or 3-hourly for most at-risk patients on a suitable surface — not routinely 4–6 hours.
  4. Reactive foam is the baseline support surface (strong); low air loss when moisture and heat contribute.
  5. Staging is unchanged, and mucosal membrane pressure injuries are still not staged.
Chapter 01 · The Edition

What Was Published
and When

The International Guideline on the Prevention and Treatment of Pressure Ulcers/Injuries is developed jointly by the National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel (NPIAP), the European Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel (EPUAP) and the Pan Pacific Pressure Injury Alliance (PPPIA). The 4th edition replaces the 2019 3rd edition.

  • February 2025 — the 4th edition launched, starting with the prevention recommendations.
  • 2025–2026 — treatment chapters followed.
  • September 2026 — the current abridged Quick Reference Guide (version date 1 September 2026) covers both prevention and treatment.

The panels have said the guideline will not be revised again until the 5th edition. That makes the 4th edition the reference point for protocols, tenders and product claims for years to come.

Chapter 02 · Biggest Change

A New Way of
Grading Advice

The most important change is methodological. For the first time the guideline uses GRADE, the system used by WHO and most modern guidelines. The old A/B1/B2/C strength-of-evidence ratings are gone. Every statement now falls into one of four types — and the wording tells you which:

WordingTypeWhat it means in practice
"We recommend…" (↑↑) / "We recommend against…" (↓↓)Strong recommendationMost or all individuals are best served by this; build it into protocol
"We suggest…" (↑)Conditional recommendationMost well-informed people would choose this, but a substantial minority would not; consider resources and preferences
"We make no recommendation on…" (↔)No recommendationBenefits and harms unclear or evidence insufficient
"It is good practice to…"Good Practice StatementFills gaps where trials are lacking; based on other evidence and expert consensus
Reading tip: Many familiar practices are now Good Practice Statements rather than graded recommendations — not because they matter less, but because randomized evidence is thin. Statements most likely to change local protocols are marked with an asterisk in the Quick Reference Guide.

Chapter 04 · Treatment

What the Treatment
Chapters Add

Suggested

NPWT for Stage 3–4

Negative pressure wound therapy is suggested to promote healing of Category/Stage 3 and 4 pressure injuries.

Conditional · very low
Suggested

Electrical stimulation

Suggested as an adjunct to standard care for Stage 2–4 and unstageable injuries — one of the better-supported treatments.

Conditional · moderate
Suggested

Healing nutrition

Energy, protein and micronutrient supplementation; a formula with protein, arginine and zinc is suggested. No recommendation on collagen supplements.

Conditional

Good Practice Statements set the treatment framework: assess the wound bed, edge and peri-wound skin; cleanse and debride as appropriate; manage infection; select dressings that balance moisture, control infection, inflammation and odor and support autolytic debridement; reassess at least weekly; and seek surgical consultation for advancing cellulitis, extensive tunneling or necrosis, or Stage 3–4 injuries that do not close. For dressing choices by wound bed, see dressings for Stage 3–4 pressure injuries.

Chapter 05 · What Didn't Change

Classification
Stays the Same

There is no new staging system. The guideline asks teams to use a classification with clearly defined categories: Category/Stage 1, 2, 3 and 4, Unstageable, and Suspected Deep Tissue Injury / Deep Tissue Pressure Injury — keeping both the European and US names. Mucosal membrane pressure injuries should not be staged. What is new is the emphasis on accuracy: teams should check that clinicians classify consistently and invest in ongoing training. See our pressure injury care guide for stage-by-stage dressing strategy.

For hospitals

Update protocols with the asterisked statements

Repositioning intervals, device-related prevention, risk screening at admission and tube-feeding policy are the most likely to change local practice.

For procurement

Read the construction, not just the category

The guideline names multilayered soft silicone foam for sacrum and heels, and reactive foam as the baseline support surface. Tender specifications can follow that wording.

For manufacturers

Claims must match the grade

Most prevention recommendations are conditional with low or very low certainty. Marketing that implies strong evidence where the guideline says "suggest" will not survive a clinical review.

For educators

Teach the four statement types

Staff who understand "recommend", "suggest", "no recommendation" and "good practice" apply the guideline with better judgment.

A Guideline Built to Last

With no revision planned before the 5th edition, the 4th edition will shape pressure-injury protocols, tenders and product claims for years. Its biggest gift is honesty: it tells clinicians exactly how strong the evidence is for each action.

For product teams, that means matching specifications to the guideline's wording — multilayer soft silicone foam where it is named, preventive dressings under devices, and claims that respect the grade.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

When was the 4th edition pressure injury guideline published?
The 4th edition International Guideline by NPIAP, EPUAP and PPPIA launched in February 2025, beginning with prevention recommendations. Treatment chapters followed, and the current Quick Reference Guide covering both is dated 1 September 2026.
What is the biggest change in the 4th edition?
The switch to GRADE methodology. Statements are now strong recommendations, conditional recommendations, no recommendation or good practice statements, replacing the A/B1/B2/C evidence ratings of the 2019 edition.
Does the new guideline recommend prophylactic dressings?
It suggests a multilayered soft silicone foam dressing on the sacrum and heels for high-risk individuals where resources permit (conditional), and strongly recommends preventive dressings under medical devices when they won't interfere with function.
How often should patients be repositioned under the 4th edition?
It suggests repositioning every two or three hours for most at-risk individuals on an appropriate pressure-redistribution surface, and suggests not routinely extending intervals to four, five or six hours, while individualizing schedules.
Did pressure injury staging change in the 2026 guideline?
No. The classification remains Category/Stage 1–4, Unstageable and Suspected Deep Tissue Injury/Deep Tissue Pressure Injury. Mucosal membrane pressure injuries should not be staged. The guideline emphasizes consistent, accurate classification.
