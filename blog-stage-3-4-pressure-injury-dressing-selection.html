Choosing the Right Dressing for
Stage III–IV Pressure Injuries

Full-thickness pressure injuries are where dressing choice matters most — and where it is most often made by habit. Here is a wound-bed-first selection matrix, the silver "two-week challenge", when to escalate to NPWT, and why cost per day beats cost per piece.

Key Takeaways

Five Things to Know in Two Minutes

  1. Choose by wound-bed condition — depth, exudate, infection, odor, tissue type — not by stage alone.
  2. Alginate or gelling fiber fills cavities loosely and handles moderate to heavy exudate.
  3. Silver is a two-week challenge for local infection, then reassess and step down.
  4. NPWT is suggested by the 2026 International Guideline for Stage 3 and 4 pressure injuries.
  5. Cost per day of care — including nursing time — is the number that matters, not price per piece.
Chapter 01 · First Principle

The Dressing Treats the Wound Bed,
Not the Stage

A Category/Stage 3 pressure injury is full-thickness skin loss with visible fat; a Stage 4 exposes fascia, muscle, tendon or bone. The stage tells you how deep the damage goes. It does not tell you which dressing to use — the condition of the wound bed today does.

The 2026 International Guideline puts it in one sentence: select wound dressings to promote an optimal healing environment "based on the condition(s) of the wound" — balance moisture, control infection and inflammation, control odor, support autolytic debridement and relieve pain. And it reminds every team that no dressing works without pressure redistribution, nutrition and regular reassessment. For staging basics, see the pressure injury care guide.

Chapter 03 · Antimicrobials

Silver: A Two-Week
Challenge, Not a Habit

Silver dressings earn their place when there are signs of local infection or high bioburden — increasing pain, exudate, odor, friable tissue, stalled healing. International consensus guidance on silver recommends a "two-week challenge": use silver for two weeks, then reassess. If the wound has improved but signs persist, continue with review; if it has resolved, step down to a non-antimicrobial dressing; if there is no improvement, rethink the plan.

Spreading cellulitis, fever or suspected bone involvement are not dressing problems. The guideline advises surgical consultation for advancing cellulitis, suspected sepsis, extensive undermining or necrosis, and Stage 3–4 injuries that do not close with standard care. For multidrug-resistant organisms, see our MRSA containment protocol.

Chapter 05 · Economics

Cost per Day,
Not Cost per Piece

Procurement often compares unit prices. Wards live with cost per day of care: the dressing price divided by the days it stays on, plus the nursing time for every change. A cheaper dressing changed daily can cost more than a premium dressing that stays on for three days.

  • Wear time is the biggest lever. Choose dressings that can safely stay on as long as the wound condition allows.
  • Leakage is expensive. An under-sized absorbent dressing that leaks forces unplanned changes and damages peri-wound skin.
  • Step down deliberately. Silver and charcoal are tools for a phase, not for the whole healing journey.

Right Tool, Right Phase

Full-thickness pressure injuries move through phases — infected, sloughy, exuding, granulating. A good dressing plan moves with them: antimicrobials for a defined period, absorbency matched to exudate, then protection that can stay on for days.

For distributors and hospital buyers, that means carrying a complete, coherent range rather than one hero product.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best dressing for a Stage 4 pressure injury?
There is no single best dressing. Choose by wound-bed condition: alginate or gelling fiber to loosely fill cavities with exudate, silver for signs of local infection, charcoal for malodor, hydrogel for slough, and silicone foam once the wound is granulating. NPWT is also suggested for Stage 3 and 4 pressure injuries.
How long should a silver dressing be used on a pressure injury?
International consensus recommends a two-week challenge: use silver for two weeks, then reassess. Continue only if signs of infection persist and the wound is improving; step down when they resolve; change the plan if there is no improvement.
Should calcium alginate be packed tightly into a cavity?
No. Alginate should fill the cavity loosely — it expands as it gels. Tight packing can create pressure on the wound bed.
Does the 2026 International Guideline recommend NPWT for pressure injuries?
Yes. The 4th edition suggests (conditional recommendation, very low certainty) negative pressure wound therapy to promote healing of Category/Stage 3 and 4 pressure injuries.
How do you compare dressing costs fairly?
Calculate cost per day of care: the dressing price divided by wear days, plus nursing time per change. Longer-wearing dressings often cost less per day despite a higher unit price.
