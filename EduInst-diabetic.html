Why Diabetic Ulcers
Are So Slow to Heal

Chronic hyperglycaemia inflicts compound damage on the nerves, blood vessels and immune system, turning a minor foot injury into a stubborn, non-healing wound. Understanding these three core mechanisms is where effective treatment begins.

Pathophysiology
⬡

The Silent Alarm

Chronic hyperglycaemia damages the longest nerve fibres first — which is why the feet, furthest from the spinal cord, tend to lose sensation before anywhere else. Up to half of people with long-standing diabetes have some degree of peripheral neuropathy, and many don't know it until a wound has already appeared. A stone in the shoe, a too-tight sock seam, or a burn from hot pavement can all go unnoticed for days.

◈

The Starved Tissue

Diabetes accelerates atherosclerosis in the small and medium arteries of the lower leg. Less blood reaching the wound means less oxygen, fewer nutrients, and fewer of the immune cells needed to fight bacteria and rebuild tissue. This is why a diabetic foot ulcer can look clean on the surface yet refuse to close — the "fuel" that healing requires simply isn't there.

◇

The Compromised Defence

Persistently high blood glucose impairs white blood cell function at a cellular level, letting bacteria that a healthy immune system would clear within days establish a foothold instead. Combined with reduced local blood flow, this is why diabetic foot infections often spread deeper and faster than expected — frequently reaching tendon or bone by the time the surface looks only mildly infected.

Staging

Wagner Classification System

The Wagner system is one of the most widely used clinical classifications for diabetic foot ulcers, grading wounds 0–5 by depth and extent of tissue necrosis to guide treatment strategy and dressing selection.

Grade 0At-risk foot: intact skin, but with neuropathy, deformity, or a prior ulcer history requiring focused prevention.
Grade 1Superficial ulcer: full-thickness skin loss, not extending into deeper subcutaneous tissue.
Grade 2Deep ulcer: extending to tendon, ligament or joint capsule, without abscess or osteomyelitis.
Grade 3Deep ulcer with abscess, osteomyelitis or joint infection.
Grade 4Localized gangrene: necrosis of the toes, heel or part of the forefoot.
Grade 5Extensive gangrene of the whole foot, usually requiring surgical intervention.
Wound Bed Preparation

The DIMES Framework

Using Wound Bed Preparation as the framework, systematically addressing every factor that affects healing.

D

Debridement

Remove necrotic tissue and callus, reduce bacterial load, and expose a healthy wound bed.

I

Infection Control

Identify and control infection and biofilm, combining antimicrobial dressings with systemic treatment where indicated.

M

Moisture Balance

Manage exudate and maintain a moist healing environment without maceration.

E

Edge Management

Promote epithelial migration at the wound edge, address undermining and edge senescence, and drive wound contraction.

S

Support

Glycaemic control, nutritional support, offloading and revascularisation — removing systemic barriers to healing.

Standard Care Pathway

Six Steps, from Assessment to Follow-Up

1

Comprehensive Assessment

Neurological, vascular and infection assessment, Wagner grading, and clarification of wound characteristics and overall condition.

2

Debridement

Removal of necrotic tissue and callus to achieve a clean wound bed and reduce bacterial load.

3

Infection Control

Topical antimicrobial dressings, combined with systemic anti-infective treatment as clinically indicated.

4

Moist Wound Healing

Foam dressing selection matched to exudate volume, locking away drainage while maintaining a moist healing environment.

5

Offloading

Offloading insoles and orthoses redistribute plantar pressure and remove ongoing mechanical stress.

6

Follow-Up & Recurrence Prevention

Glycaemic management, regular foot examinations and properly fitted footwear to reduce recurrence risk.

Home Prevention

Seven Points for
Daily Foot Care

The vast majority of diabetic foot ulcers begin with a small, detectable injury. A consistent daily check and proper care routine is the lowest-cost, highest-return intervention available.

Seek prompt medical care if you notice
  • A break in the skin, blister, redness, warmth or swelling on the foot
  • Increased wound drainage, odour, or discolouration of surrounding skin
  • Sudden worsening of foot pain, or an existing wound with no improvement in two weeks
  • An ingrown toenail, worsening athlete's foot, or extensive dry, cracked skin
  • Inspect both feet daily in good light, including the soles and between the toes — use a mirror or ask for help if needed.
  • Wash feet in warm water (test with your elbow, no hotter than 37°C) and dry gently, especially between the toes.
  • Moisturise the top and sole of the foot to prevent cracking, but avoid between the toes to prevent maceration.
  • Trim toenails straight across, avoiding the corners; leave corns and calluses to a professional.
  • Never walk barefoot; wear well-fitted, breathable shoes and socks indoors and out; check inside shoes for foreign objects before wearing.
  • Avoid direct heat sources such as hot water bottles or electric blankets on the feet — reduced sensation makes burns easy to miss.
  • Follow medical advice to manage blood glucose, blood pressure and lipids, stop smoking, and attend regular professional foot screening.
