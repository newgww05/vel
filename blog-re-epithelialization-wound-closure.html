Re-Epithelialization
When Is the Wound Truly Closed?

The day a wound closes is the day most care stops — and the day the tissue underneath is at its most fragile. Here is what the skin is still doing at day 7, 14 and 28, why healed tissue never quite regains full strength, and how to dress for that invisible work.

Key Takeaways

Five Things to Know in Two Minutes

  1. "Closed" means covered — 100% re-epithelialization without drainage — not fully healed.
  2. New epidermis is thin and leaky; it shears and tears easily for weeks.
  3. Healed tissue is weaker: about 20% of normal strength at three weeks, about 80% at best.
  4. Chronic wounds recur: about 40% of diabetic foot ulcers return within a year.
  5. Dress for the invisible work — silicone protection, barrier care, offloading and scar management.
Chapter 01 · Definition

What "Closed"
Actually Means

Re-epithelialization is the process by which new epidermal cells migrate across a wound and cover it. When the surface is fully covered and there is no drainage, most clinical definitions call the wound closed — commonly confirmed as 100% re-epithelialization without drainage, often on two visits about two weeks apart in clinical studies.

That is the moment the dressing usually comes off and the chart says "healed". Underneath, the work is far from finished. The new epidermis is thin, the barrier leaks water, and the dermis below is still rebuilding its collagen framework — a remodeling phase that continues for months and, in larger wounds, up to a year or more.

Day 7

A barrier that still leaks

The new epidermis is only a few cell layers thick, with an immature stratum corneum. Transepidermal water loss stays high and the skin is easily sheared by tape removal or friction.

Protect from shear and adhesives
Day 14

Collagen is being rebuilt

Fibroblasts lay down collagen and begin replacing early type III with stronger type I. The scar can be red, raised and itchy — the window when scar management begins.

Start scar care once fully closed
Day 28

Strong-looking, not strong

Classic studies show tensile strength reaches only around 20% of normal at three weeks. Even after a year, scar tissue reaches roughly 80% at best.

The last number explains why "closed" is a dangerous word for chronic wounds. A healed diabetic foot ulcer or pressure injury leaves scar tissue that is weaker, less elastic and less perfused than the skin around it. If the cause — pressure, shear, poor footwear, poor perfusion — returns, the wound returns, often at the same site. Clinicians increasingly describe these patients as being "in remission" rather than cured.

Error 01

Stopping all protection at closure

Fresh epithelium is the most fragile skin on the body. Removing every dressing on day one invites tears and re-opening.

Error 02

Aggressive adhesive tapes

Stripping an acrylic tape off new skin can pull away the epidermis that took weeks to form. Use soft silicone adhesives.

Error 03

Ignoring the original cause

A pressure injury that closed while the patient was turned every two hours will reopen if turning stops at discharge.

Error 04

Starting silicone scar sheets too early

Silicone scar products go on closed, dry skin — not on open wounds or weeping incisions.

Did you know? Re-epithelialization can be watched in real time in moist wounds: epidermal cells migrate faster across a moist surface than under a dry scab — the foundation of modern moist wound healing.

Healing Doesn't End at Closure

Re-epithelialization is a milestone, not a finish line. The tissue beneath a closed wound is still rebuilding, still fragile and — for chronic wounds — still exposed to the cause that created it.

A short, deliberate aftercare plan protects weeks of healing work: gentle adhesives, barrier support, offloading and timely scar care.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

When is a wound considered closed?
Most clinical definitions call a wound closed when it is 100% re-epithelialized with no drainage. Clinical studies often confirm this on two visits about two weeks apart.
How long does it take for healed skin to regain strength?
Strength returns slowly. Classic studies show about 20% of normal tensile strength at three weeks, and a mature scar reaches about 80% of normal at best after many months of remodeling.
Why do pressure injuries and diabetic foot ulcers come back?
Healed tissue is weaker and less elastic than normal skin, and if the original cause — pressure, shear or poor perfusion — returns, the wound often recurs at the same site. About 40% of diabetic foot ulcers recur within one year.
Should a dressing be used after a wound has closed?
Often yes, for a period. A soft silicone dressing protects fragile new skin from shear and adhesive trauma, and prophylactic foam can protect former pressure-injury sites.
When can silicone scar sheets be used?
Once the wound surface is fully closed and dry — not on open wounds or weeping incisions.
