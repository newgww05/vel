The Cost of Prevention
vs the Cost of Cure

A full-thickness pressure injury can cost tens of thousands of dollars to treat. The dressing that helps prevent it costs a few. The math is not subtle — but budgets see the dressing line item, not the trajectory. Here is how to connect the two in a business case anyone can audit.

Key Takeaways

Five Things to Know in Two Minutes

  1. One hospital-acquired pressure ulcer costs an estimated $20,900–$151,700 to treat in the US.
  2. Prevention cost is visible; treatment cost is dispersed across bed-days, departments and penalties.
  3. A five-input business case connects both columns and can be rebuilt with any hospital's data.
  4. Even with conservative assumptions, avoided cost is many times the prevention spend.
  5. Target high-risk patients and don't forget preventive dressings under medical devices.

Cost estimates vary by country, setting and method, but the pattern is consistent everywhere: a full-thickness pressure injury (Category/Stage 3 or 4) is one of the most expensive avoidable complications in hospital care. The bill comes from longer stays, specialist dressings and support surfaces, nursing time, treatment of infection, surgery for the worst cases — and, in many health systems, financial penalties or non-payment for hospital-acquired conditions.

US analyses consistently find that the most severe injuries account for a disproportionate share of total cost. One deep sacral injury can cost more than a year of prevention dressings for an entire ward.

Chapter 02 · The Visibility Problem

Why Budgets See the Dressing,
Not the Trajectory

This is the structural reason prevention is underfunded. The ward manager who buys prophylactic dressings carries the cost; the savings land in bed-day budgets, surgical departments and the legal office. Making the business case means connecting those two columns in one calculation.

Chapter 03 · The Math

A Simple, Honest
Business Case

The calculation needs only five inputs. Here is an illustrative example for a 20-bed critical-care unit — replace every number with your own data:

InputIllustrative valueWhere to get yours
High-risk admissions per year800Admissions × share assessed as high risk
Baseline pressure injury incidence10%Your own audit or incidence data
Relative reduction with the prevention bundle50%Published trials; be conservative
Average cost per pressure injury$20,000Finance department; use the low end of published ranges if unsure
Prevention cost per patient$30Dressings for the stay + extra staff time

In this example, $24,000 of prevention avoids about $800,000 of treatment cost. You can halve every benefit assumption and the case still holds. The Border Trial in critical care, for instance, reported sacral and heel pressure injuries in 3.1% of patients with prophylactic multilayer foam versus 13.1% without — a far larger reduction than the 50% used here. Chapter 04 · Making It Stick

Four Moves for
Budget Owners

Measure

Know your baseline

Run a point-prevalence audit and track hospital-acquired incidence by stage. You cannot prove savings without a starting number.

Audit quarterly
Target

Spend where risk is highest

The 2026 International Guideline targets prophylactic silicone foam at high-risk individuals — critical care, long surgery, very low mobility.

Risk-based, not blanket
Report

Put both columns on one page

Present prevention spend next to avoided injuries and their estimated cost, signed off with finance.

Shared ownership
Trap 01

Comparing unit prices

A cheaper foam that wrinkles, lifts or must be changed daily can cost more per patient-day than a better-engineered one.

Trap 02

Cutting prevention after a good quarter

Low incidence is the result of prevention, not a reason to stop it. Rates climb back within months.

Trap 03

Dressings without the bundle

Dressings are one part. Risk assessment, support surfaces, repositioning, nutrition and skin care deliver the result together.

Trap 04

Ignoring device-related injuries

The 2026 guideline strongly recommends preventive dressings under medical devices where they don't interfere with function — a cheap, often-missed win.

Make the Trajectory Visible

Nobody needs convincing that prevention is cheaper than cure. What budget owners need is the calculation in front of them — with their own baseline, their own costs, and both columns on the same page.

Once that page exists, prophylactic dressings stop being a line item to cut and become what they are: one of the best-value purchases in the ward.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a pressure injury cost to treat?
US estimates from AHRQ put the cost of care per hospital-acquired pressure ulcer at about $20,900 to $151,700, with the most severe, full-thickness injuries at the high end. Costs differ by country and setting.
Is pressure injury prevention cost-effective?
In most analyses, yes. Prevention costs per patient are small compared with treatment costs, so even modest reductions in incidence usually save far more than they cost.
Why is pressure injury prevention often underfunded?
Prevention costs appear as visible line items in one budget, while treatment costs are spread across bed-days, departments, penalties and litigation, so savings are rarely attributed back to prevention.
How do I build a business case for prophylactic dressings?
Use five inputs: high-risk admissions per year, baseline incidence, expected relative reduction, average cost per injury and prevention cost per patient. Compare avoided treatment cost with prevention spend.
Which patients should receive prophylactic dressings?
The 2026 International Guideline suggests multilayer soft silicone foam on the sacrum and heels for individuals assessed as high risk, and strongly recommends preventive dressings under medical devices where they don't interfere with function.
