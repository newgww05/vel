Hypertrophic Scar Control
After Surgical Closure

Most of a scar's final shape is decided in two windows: the first 72 hours and the first six months. Here is what drives a raised scar, when silicone gel sheets help (and when they don't), how long to use them, and the signals that it is time to escalate.

Key Takeaways

Five Things to Know in Two Minutes

  1. Tension is the strongest driver of hypertrophic scars; closure technique and edge support come first.
  2. Silicone is first-line prevention — but only on a fully closed, dry incision, usually from about week two.
  3. Wear it 12+ hours a day for at least two to three months, longer while the scar is red or raised.
  4. Gel for moving areas, sheets under clothing; paper tape adds tension offloading.
  5. Escalate early if the scar is still raised at three to six months or spreads beyond the incision.
Chapter 01 · The Problem

Why Some Incisions
Heal Raised

A hypertrophic scar is a raised, often red and itchy scar that stays within the borders of the original incision. It forms when the remodeling phase overshoots: fibroblasts keep producing collagen, and the collagen is laid down in dense, disorganized nodules instead of a flat, orderly sheet.

Three factors push an incision toward hypertrophy:

  • Tension across the wound — the strongest mechanical driver. Chest, shoulders, joints and the lower abdomen are classic high-tension zones.
  • Prolonged inflammation — infection, delayed healing or wound breakdown extend the inflammatory phase.
  • Individual predisposition — younger age, darker skin types and a personal or family history of raised scars.
Chapter 02 · The First 72 Hours

Scar Control Starts
at Closure

No scar product is used on an open incision. In the first days, scar prevention means giving the wound the best possible start:

  1. Tension-free closure — layered suturing that takes the load off the skin edges is the surgeon's main scar-prevention tool.
  2. Support the edges with skin closure strips over a subcuticular suture or after early staple removal, so the wound is not pulled apart by movement.
  3. Keep it protected and clean under a waterproof film island dressing that allows showering and does not shear the incision on removal.
  4. Prevent infection and breakdown — every day of extra inflammation is a day of extra collagen.
Chapter 03 · The First Six Months

Silicone: When It Helps,
When It Doesn't

International clinical recommendations on scar management name silicone gel sheets or silicone gel as the first-line, non-invasive option for preventing hypertrophic scars after surgery. Silicone works through occlusion and hydration of the outer skin layer, which signals the underlying fibroblasts to slow collagen production — the scar becomes softer, flatter and less itchy.

How long, how often? Most recommendations advise wearing silicone for at least 12 hours a day, ideally longer, for a minimum of two to three months — and continuing while the scar remains red or raised. Consistency matters more than any single product feature. Velcura's scar management range offers both sheets for incisions under clothing and a fast-drying gel for the face and joints.

Tension offloading works alongside silicone. Microporous paper tape applied along a C-section or abdominal incision for several weeks has been shown in a randomized trial to reduce scar volume, by keeping the wound edges from being pulled apart during everyday movement.

Chapter 04 · Escalation

When to Refer —
and to What

Signal

Still raised at 3–6 months

Despite consistent silicone use and tension control, the scar stays thick, red or itchy.

Specialist review
Signal

Spreading beyond the incision

Growth past the original borders suggests a keloid, not a hypertrophic scar.

Dermatology / plastics
Signal

Pain, contracture, restricted movement

Scars across joints or with functional impact need active treatment.

Early referral

Specialist options include intralesional corticosteroid injections, laser therapy (for example pulsed-dye laser for redness), pressure therapy and, for selected cases, surgical revision — usually combined with continued silicone. The earlier a problem scar is escalated, the more options remain open.

Two Windows, One Plan

Hypertrophic scars are among the most preventable complications of surgery. A tension-free closure, a protected first week, and consistent silicone through the first months of remodeling change the outcome for most at-risk patients.

For product teams, the opportunity is a complete pathway: closure strips and waterproof dressings at discharge, then silicone sheets and gel for the months that follow.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

When should silicone scar sheets be started after surgery?
Once the incision is fully closed and dry, with sutures or staples removed — typically around two weeks after surgery. Never apply silicone to an open or weeping wound.
How long should silicone gel sheets be worn each day?
Most recommendations advise at least 12 hours a day, ideally longer, for a minimum of two to three months, continuing while the scar remains red or raised.
Do silicone sheets work on old scars?
They work best on scars that are still remodeling, generally under a year old. Mature, pale, flat scars benefit little.
What is the difference between a hypertrophic scar and a keloid?
A hypertrophic scar stays within the incision borders and may flatten over time. A keloid grows beyond the original borders, rarely regresses and often recurs after excision, so it needs specialist treatment.
Does taping an incision reduce scarring?
Tension offloading helps. A randomized trial found that microporous paper tape applied along surgical incisions for several weeks reduced hypertrophic scar volume.
